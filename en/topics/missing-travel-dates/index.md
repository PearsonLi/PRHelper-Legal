<h2>The problem is uncertainty, not memory</h2>
<p>A blank period often creates pressure to choose a date that “looks right.” That is the wrong goal. A useful reconstruction keeps known dates, uncertain dates, and supporting sources visibly separate until the evidence is strong enough to close the gap.</p>

<section class="story-panel">
  <p class="story-kicker">Illustrative scenario</p>
  <h2>One weekend disappeared between two calendars.</h2>
  <p>Maya remembers driving to Buffalo in autumn. Her passport has no stamp, the hotel receipt shows Saturday, and a photo is dated Sunday. Instead of inventing the border dates, she records the trip as unresolved and works outward from the evidence.</p>
</section>

<h2>Use a source-first recovery order</h2>
<ol>
  <li><strong>Build the known timeline first.</strong> Enter trips with reliable departure and return dates before investigating gaps.</li>
  <li><strong>Flag uncertainty.</strong> Keep a short list of periods that still need verification.</li>
  <li><strong>Check primary records.</strong> Review current and expired passports, airline or rail accounts, booking emails, and official travel-history sources.</li>
  <li><strong>Check independent secondary records.</strong> Calendars, geotagged photos, card transactions, roaming records, work schedules, and school records can narrow a period.</li>
  <li><strong>Resolve one gap at a time.</strong> Record which source supports the final date so it can be reviewed later.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Avoid three common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
  <li>Assuming a booking date proves the trip actually occurred.</li>
  <li>Using only passport stamps for Canada–US land travel.</li>
  <li>Changing one date without checking whether it creates an overlap elsewhere.</li>
</ul>

<div class="callout"><strong>Application check:</strong> Follow the current IRCC application guide and official calculator. If the missing period could affect eligibility or involves an exception, obtain advice from a licensed professional.</div>
